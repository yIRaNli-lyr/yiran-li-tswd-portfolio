# Final Project Part III: Can Books Still Compete for Our Attention?

[Read the published Shorthand story](https://carnegiemellon.shorthandstories.com/can-books-still-compete-for-our-attention/index.html)

The final story examines how reading competes for a limited share of young adults' leisure time. It begins with my experience of losing patience while reading, then moves through three questions: which age group reads the least, where young adults' leisure time goes, and whether the pattern appears within a longer national shift in reading participation.

# Changes made since Part II

The most important change was the story order. Earlier versions presented the charts without a strong progression. Feedback from my interviews made me reconsider what each chart contributed and how one idea should lead to the next. The final sequence moves from a personal moment to an age comparison, then to the distribution of young adults' leisure time, and finally to a national reading trend. I also replaced one dataset because the earlier evidence did not focus closely enough on young adults.

The final version uses interactive Tableau charts in place of preliminary images. I updated the age comparison and rebuilt the leisure-time visual with 2025 American Time Use Survey data for people ages 15 to 24. The charts share one green accent, restrained gray tones, consistent sources, and a pale green background. This gives the story a quieter visual style and keeps attention on reading.

I also simplified the Shorthand presentation. Each data section pairs one chart with a short explanation, while the opening connects the topic to a familiar reading experience. Shorthand's layout controls were the most difficult part of production. The current format is concise and direct, although its spacing and responsive layout could be refined further.

## Audience and focus

The primary audience is young adults who want to read yet struggle to remain patient with long-form text. Entertainment is abundant, immediate, and easy to access. A book asks for more time before it offers the same sense of reward. This contrast helped me narrow the project from the broad question of why people no longer read or buy physical books to a measurable question about reading within limited leisure time.

Defining this audience changed the evidence I selected. The age chart establishes that Americans ages 15 to 24 spend the least time reading for personal interest. The waffle chart then shows how the same age group's leisure time is divided. The national trend provides context without presenting it as a measure of young adults alone.

## How user research shaped the story

I showed the Shorthand draft to three master's students in class. The waffle chart was immediately understandable, so I retained it. One participant recommended making the young adult group more visually prominent. Another found the progression among the three charts unclear. That response had the greatest effect on the final version because it led me to revise the narrative order and reconsider the supporting data.

The final design highlights ages 15 to 24 in green, places the age comparison first, and follows it with the leisure-time breakdown. The long-term trend appears last as national context. Short headings turn the sequence into three connected questions: who reads the least, where their time goes, and whether the result belongs to a broader shift.

## Final design decisions

- **Personal opening:** The electronic-book image introduces the subject through a moment of lost patience rather than an abstract statistic.
- **Age comparison:** A vertical bar chart makes the difference among age groups quick to scan and gives the youngest group a clear visual emphasis.
- **Leisure-time breakdown:** A 100-cell waffle chart shows the small share assigned to reading without requiring readers to compare several axes.
- **Long-term context:** A line chart shows the change in national book-reading participation from 2012 to 2022.
- **Visual system:** Black text, gray supporting marks, and green `#228B22` create a consistent hierarchy. The pale green background connects the charts to the electronic-book opening.
- **Writing:** Each section uses a question and a short explanation so that readers who have limited patience can move through the story quickly.

## What I learned

The audience and intended message need to be defined at the beginning of a project. A collection of relevant charts does not automatically create a clear story. The order of the evidence determines what readers understand and which questions they carry into the next section.

I am most satisfied with the final story's simplicity and force. It communicates one idea without overloading the reader. With more time, I would improve the Shorthand layout across different screen sizes and refine the balance between the charts and surrounding text.
